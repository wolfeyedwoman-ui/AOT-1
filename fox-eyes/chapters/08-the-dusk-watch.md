# Chapter 8 — The Dusk Watch

She woke to the tower going amber, the courtyard sounds shifting to the dusk watch. And then, over the last of the light:

Two raps. One knuckle. Unhurried, evenly spaced — the cadence of a man auditing a door he already knew was bolted, exactly as promised, checking on an order he'd given twice.

And between Súile and that cadence sat one small bookkeeping matter: *every contact reported* — and a doorstep delivery to the asset, made and slept on, currently on nobody's desk but hers.

She pulled back the bolt, opened the door, and bowed low with one arm extended. "Welcome in, Captain."

Levi looked at the bow — the full theatrical sweep of it, arm extended, curls falling forward — the way he looked at Erwin's more baroque expedition budgets. He stepped past her into the room.

"Tch. Two days of insubordination and *now* protocol." He said it to the shelves, already sweeping the room out of incurable habit — hearth, window, floor, bootless boots by the door where a rail would be if towers had them. "The bow goes on the list. Under *T*, for theater."

But the audit was already running under the flatness, and she could watch it clear her: color back in her face, the shoulder swinging loose when she'd pulled the door, the bed actually slept in — *horizontal, confirmed, order fulfilled on the third telling*, filed with the special grudging stamp he reserved for compliance that arrived late but complete. He'd come from dusk rounds with the day still on him, cravat exact, eyes shadowed in the way she'd learned wasn't tiredness — the man ran on two hours and a chair; the shadows were standard issue, like the blades.

He set a small teapot on her apothecary table. He'd brought it across the keep under one arm — the good pot, the leaves already in it, a thing that had absolutely not happened for anyone else in the history of the Scout Regiment. "You have the honey," he said, as if completing a supply manifest. "I brought the rest."

Then, mid-motion, pouring water from her kettle by the fire, without any change of tone at all:

"Your vinegar bottle's missing."

A beat. He didn't look up from the pour. "Third shelf, left of the fox tales, between the dried yarrow and the tincture you mislabel on purpose. There last night. Gone tonight. Along with the soda tin you keep for the copper." Now he looked up — grey, level, and not one degree angry, which was somehow considerably worse and, worse still, very slightly *amused*. He settled into the old study chair by the hearth, the one that had quietly become his the way the honey dish had become hers, and held his cup in that odd way, palm around the rim, and let the steam rise between them.

"Mina's feather's crooked," he added, nodding at the shelf, entirely conversational. "Suits the shelf." A slow sip. The eyes came back to her, patient as winter, and one degree warmer than the audit strictly required.

"Report while it steeps, Fox Eyes. All of it. And then we'll discuss what a *contact* is, since apparently my definitions want footnotes."

She debriefed the way she mapped: clean lines, no flourish. The door that opened too fast. The parade-standard kit. The spirits riding the warm air at midday, day two. The touch that landed on tin. The debt recontracted before she'd reached the stairs — *I'll return the tin* — and, through the thin wood, the listening pause and the glass on glass.

Levi absorbed it into the ledger with his cup at his lips, and gave back the synthesis in the working register, quiet against the fire-crack.

"A drunk." He said it with something close to appreciation — a craftsman finding the flaw line in a stone. "Parade kit, pressed cuffs, and a bottle in the boot locker on his second day, before anyone's shot at him. That's not celebration drinking, that's *maintenance* drinking. Whoever holds his leash sent a cracked tool into a long game." A slow sip. "That's our second weak plank, Fox Eyes — the farrier hates his master, and this one drinks at his own performance. Every barn has one. Sometimes the barn *is* one. A sober spy waits. A drunk one gets impatient for his payoff, and impatience is the only informant that never lies." He tipped the cup a fraction toward her. "The tin comes back — you don't take that meeting alone in a corridor, you take it in the mess at full noon. Make him do his tradecraft in a crowd. Drunks hate an audience they can't work."

Then she set down her spoon and said the other thing.

"I heard you in the corridor. One floorboard. You wanted to be known."

Levi went very still, the way the ridge had taught her he went still: not caught. *Placing* something.

"Tch." He set the cup down with a click that in a softer man would have been a laugh. "Four steps. It took you four steps to make me, and you finished the conversation anyway, and then you sat on it through a nap, a bow, and a shelf inspection you knew was redundant — and let me perform it." The grey eyes narrowed a millimeter; on him it was a standing ovation. "You let the cat do his little routine with the vinegar bottle. Out of *courtesy*."

He leaned back in the chair that had become his, fingers laced, and gave her the accounting, flat and unhurried, because she'd earned the itemization.

"Yes. I wanted to be known. Three reasons, alphabetized." One finger. "*Backup.* My order says no quiet corners, and a barracks doorstep at siesta is a quiet corner with a latch. You bent the order into something useful, so instead of countermanding good work I stood where I could reach him in under a second if the charm slipped. You were never at that door alone. You'll never be at any of his doors alone." A second finger. "*Bookkeeping.* You needed to know that contacts get observed — that the report you owe me is a formality, not my only source. You filed clean tonight. Noted." A third finger, and here the register dropped to the one the mess hall never got: "*Signature.* I don't watch you anonymous, Sergeant. I'm not him at a window glass, and I'm not the Interior at a keyhole. If I'm ever behind you in a corridor, you'll get the floorboard. That's the arrangement. That's always been the arrangement."

He let that sit exactly as long as it needed to. Then, because the room was getting warm in a way that had nothing to do with the hearth, he reached two fingers into his breast pocket and extracted — a quarter-inch, no more, just the corner showing — a folded paper. Creased soft with carrying. Ruled lines visible through the weave.

"And before you decide the list is a figure of speech." He tapped it once and filed it away again. "Page four has your bow on it already. I wrote it on the stairs."

He poured her cup, dosed the honey, uninvited, exact, and pushed it across the apothecary table to her side. Outside, full dark had settled over the west tower; below, faint, the sounds of the keep banking itself for the night.

"Drink your tea, Fox Eyes."

She picked up the cup, walked to the door, and bolted it — *clack* — and Levi's eyebrow acknowledged the premeditation the way a border post acknowledges an army: noted, logged, too late. She came back, sipped her tea, set the cup on the table — and draped her arms around his neck, kissing his ear.

The flick of tongue at the lobe was precisely calibrated to consume one hundred percent of a man's attention for one half of one second — even this man's, even this attention — and that, of course, was the price of admission. She stepped back with his list in her hand.

Levi looked down at his breast pocket. Then at the paper. Then at her. The grey eyes did something complicated and settled on a kind of stunned professional reverence.

"I felt the tongue," he said, flat, "and not the fingers." A beat. "That trade is going in your file. *Both halves.*"

She was already reading — arms recrossed his way, weight on her heels his way, chin down his way, and the impression was, he had to concede, *devastating*: the exact metronome flatness, the pause before the noun. And the list gave her everything she needed, because the list was real, and it was *exactly* what it threatened to be — four small pages in handwriting like upright rows of pins, ruthlessly alphabetized, cross-referenced:

*"Bow, theatrical. See: Theater."*
*"Dimples, weaponized, two (2). Repeat offense."*
*"Eye roll — cumulative. Running tab."*
*"Handkerchief, 'roof tile' remark. Insolent. Accurate."*
*"Honey, spite-based refusal of."*
*"Hips, invocation of, in the presence of a child."*
*"'Mostly.'"* — an entry that was nothing but the one word in quotation marks, which in context was somehow the most damning line on the page —
*"Orders, creative accounting of. See: Sleep. See also: Everything."*

And then her recital slowed, because page two was different. One entry, alone, underlined twice, the pen pressed hard enough to shadow through the paper:

*"Recklessness, solo, within rifle range, in lightning. — Never again without me."*

Not a grievance. A vow, filed under R, hiding in an accounting system because that was the only language its author fully trusted. She read it, and didn't mime it, and the fire cracked once into the quiet.

The last page. The last entry. Fresh ink — last night's ink:

*"Weary — 'too weary to fight.' Rain check held. Honored on demand."*

"You should see your face," Levi said quietly, from the chair. "You thought you stole my list. You stole an *IOU*."

He stood. He did it the way he did everything — no hurry, no waste — and undid the cravat with two fingers, folded it in half, in quarters, and set it on the apothecary table next to the honey dish: the formal signature of the Scout Regiment's shortest, most dangerous man accepting terms. He rolled one shoulder. Somewhere under the flat expression, way down, something that had grown up fighting in the Underground for bread and been taught by the worst man in the world how to never lose was — *delighted*.

"Feet squared. Weight forward. Thief's guard." He read her stance the way he read maps, circling one slow step to the left, hands loose. "Kenny the Ripper taught me in a tunnel with a knife, brat. You'll get the polite version." A pause, one degree warmer. "Demand received."

She hit him like a snare going off — fast, low, *honestly* fast, fox-quick fingers going for the folded paper he'd tucked back in his pocket because of course the list was the prize, it was always going to be the list — and the polite version turned out to be a hand that wasn't where it had been, a wrist redirected with terrifying gentleness, two bodies pivoting once around a point in the firelight like a compass finding north. It ended — the opening exchange, the *first* of what the night's ledger would record as several — with her wrist folded flat to his chest, his other hand fenced light at her waist, her free hand gripping his collar, both of them a breath apart and neither of them, on the record, losing.

The corner of Levi's mouth moved four percent. On him, a standing ovation.

"Tch," he said, softly, to the yellow eyes at close range. "*There's* the fox."

She bit his chest — not hard, but with hunger — and pivoted them back toward the bed, and the last thing the firelight witnessed was the two of them turning as one body before the tower drew its stone shoulder across the doorway of the scene.

The fire burned down unhurried, the way good fires do. The jay feather stood its crooked watch on the shelf beside nine old stories about clever spirits and the proud men they collect, and the bolt stayed thrown, and the tower kept the rest the way the tower kept everything — off the record, off the list, and absolutely, alphabetically *filed*.
