# Chapter 3 — The Fox Lady and the Barn Cat

The rain had settled into a steady drum by the time they crossed the courtyard, Levi's lamp throwing a swinging circle of light ahead of them. In the stables, a broad-shouldered man in a soaked homespun coat was rubbing down a plow horse with fistfuls of straw, working like a man who badly needed his hands busy. He came to a sort of half-bow, half-salute at the sight of Levi's jacket — then his eyes caught the wings on it and went wide.

"You're — this is *Scouts*, then. Sir. Wallis, sir. Tenant farmer, out past the west fork, half a day toward Rose." He wrung the straw in his hands. "Wheel went at the worst stretch of the road. If your sergeant hadn't come along—" he nodded at Súile with the particular helpless gratitude of a father, "—we'd have been under that wagon till morning. She put my Mina on her own horse. Rode the last hour with lightning coming down like judgment day."

"Did she." Levi's glance slid sideways to Súile — *rode double through a lightning storm, that's going in the conversation later* — and back. "Your wagon's being fetched at first light. You'll be on the road by noon."

In the small guardhouse room, a fire was already snapping, and Petra was crouched by the hearth in front of a girl of six or seven cocooned in a grey regulation blanket, coaxing her through a bowl of stew with the gentle patience of someone born for it. "—and it's the *oldest* castle inside the wall, so it's been through hundreds of storms worse than this one," she was saying warmly. She rose smoothly at Levi's entrance. "Captain. They're settled. I gave them the second bread ration, I'll note it in the book myself." Her smile at him was pure and bright; the version Súile received, one degree later, had the warmth of a supply invoice.

The girl, Mina, peered out of her blanket — past Levi, past Petra — and lit up at Súile like the storm had never happened.

"It's the fox lady!" she announced to the room, with a six-year-old's total confidence in the importance of her news. "Papa, look, it's her, the one with the yellow eyes!" Then, having studied the room's arrangement — small scowling captain, holding the lamp, standing unmistakably at the fox lady's shoulder — she tilted her head and asked, at devastating volume:

"Is the little grumpy one your husband?"

The fire popped. Petra went absolutely still, ladle in hand. Somewhere behind them in the doorway, Wallis made a sound like a man swallowing his own soul.

Levi's expression did not move a single millimeter. He raised his cup — he had, somehow, brought his tea across the courtyard — and took a slow sip.

"Answer carefully, Sergeant," he said, flat as slate. "There's a child present."

"Ha!" Súile laughed, covered her mouth. "He's my Captain, dear. I report to him. I follow his orders, mostly." She shot a side-eyed glance at Levi, then looked back at the girl. "How are you feeling? Ever stayed in a castle before?"

"*Mostly*," Levi repeated, to no one, in the tone of a man entering a debt into a ledger.

Mina was already shaking her head, curls bouncing. "Never ever. Our house has one room and Papa's boots live in it." She looked up at the smoke-dark beams and dared, in a whisper: "Are there *ghosts?*"

"No ghosts," Levi said flatly. "They wouldn't pass inspection."

The girl absorbed this with enormous seriousness. Then, with the guileless aim of a child artillery battery, she fired again. "Papa says the Scouts are the brave ones that go outside the Walls and don't come back, and that's why we shouldn't stare when they ride past looking sad." Wallis made his second mortally wounded sound of the evening and began apologizing into his hands, but Mina pressed on, studying Levi doubtfully over the rim of her stew bowl. "But you're not sad. You're just small and cross. Are you *really* the captain? Súile's bigger than you."

"She is not—" Petra was upright before anyone saw her move, ladle raised like a sabre, devotion blazing straight through her manners. "Mina, the Captain is *humanity's strongest soldier*. He's worth an entire brigade. Soldiers tell stories about him. He is the finest—" she caught herself mid-hymn, having heard her own volume, and finished with dignity, "—officer in the Scout Regiment."

"Petra." Levi hadn't moved. "The kid's six. She's counting in boots." He crouched — coming level with Mina's eyes, forearms on his knees, tea still balanced in one palm. When he spoke again it was the same flat voice, but slower. "Your papa's half right. We go outside so that one day a kid like you can stand on that Wall and be bored by the view. Some of us don't come back. The ones who do—" his eyes flicked, briefly, to Súile, "—stop for broken wagons. So eat your stew and don't worry about the sad ones. That's my job."

"Mina," Súile said, smirking. "I'm about eight centimeters shorter than the Captain. What do you mean I'm bigger?" She pointed at her hips. "Is it these?"

For a moment the room hung in perfect suspense — Wallis frozen mid-mortification, Petra frozen mid-ladle — while Mina subjected the question, and the indicated hips, to the full solemn rigor of six-year-old peer review.

"No," she ruled at last, with a small frown at being so misunderstood. "Your *hair* is bigger. It goes—" both hands escaped the blanket to mime an explosion around her head, "—*whoomf*. And you're louder. And you take up more of the room." She pointed her spoon at Levi by way of contrast. "He's little and quiet like the cat that lives in our barn. It also doesn't like mud."

"Tch." Levi took this assessment with total equanimity — being compared to a barn cat visibly landed as the finest compliment he'd received all year — but his eyes had already cut to Súile, flat and grey and promising consequences. "Oi. *There's a kid present*, Sergeant." Her own sermon, returned to sender at full deadpan. "That's two explanations you owe me now. I'm starting a list. It has a heading."

Petra, meanwhile, had gone through several expressions in close order — scandal on the Captain's behalf, then a brief, involuntary downward glance at her own trim, regulation-standard silhouette, then a swift and furious return to stirring. "*Some* of us," she observed to the stew, sweetly, "keep our figures out of conversations with children."

"Some of us," Levi said, already at the door, "were leaving. Wallis — noon. Mina—" he paused. The girl looked up at him, spoon in her mouth. Barn-cat and battery had apparently made their peace. "The fox lady's right about one thing. You were brave in the storm. Now sleep."

Then the door was open and the rain was loud and he was holding the lamp out to Súile, which meant *walk with me* in a language he'd never once spoken aloud. The courtyard was black and silver, water sheeting off the eaves. Halfway across, without turning his head, his voice came low under the rain — that other register, the one that never made it into the mess hall:

"Ten centimeters. You gave yourself two extra just to bait me in front of an audience." A beat. "Office. Report first — the real one, Fox Eyes, the west road, whatever kept your detail's course crooked before the wagon did. I read the duty map. You were off-route *before* the storm hit."

He pushed open the office door and stood aside to let her pass, eyes narrow, waiting.

"Then the list."
