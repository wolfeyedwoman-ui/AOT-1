# Chapter 5 — The West Tower

She rolled her eyes, moved the boots to the rail, and went.

In her quarters, she set to bathing. She had fetched a pail of hot water from the kitchen boiler and hauled it up the spiraling stairs of the tower herself, careful not to spill. She set it down by the lit hearth now, undressed, and began washing the mud off her skin with a rag and the lavender-oatmeal soap she made herself.

Her door was unbolted. Her room sat on the opposite side of the keep from the other officers' quarters. She had chosen it for the view. It had also been full of contraband books when she found it — a forgotten room off the west tower that had served as a library study and apothecary during more prosperous times. The shelves were lined with rotting books on pre-Wall mythology and folklore. No one knew she'd read every single one. The air smelled of damp herbs.

The knock, when it came, was two raps of one knuckle — unhurried, evenly spaced, a cadence no one else in the regiment used because no one else in the regiment knocked like they were auditing the door. Then, unhurried, it opened, the way it only ever did on the far side of the keep, where the corridor went nowhere else and the bolt was never thrown.

"Finish what you're doing." Levi came in sideways, a tray balanced on one hand, and pointedly gave her and her pail of bathwater his back. "I'm inspecting your shelves, not you. One scandal per evening. The kid used up our allotment."

The tray went onto the little apothecary table — teapot, two cups, and, set down with the gravity of a peace treaty, Petra's honey dish. *You skipped the honey out of spite*, said the arrangement. *Spite is inefficient*, said the second cup.

He stood before the shelves with his hands clasped behind him, in parade rest, and read spines by firelight while the rag and the lavender-oatmeal soap did their work behind him. The rain worried at the tower window. The room breathed its smell of damp herbs and old paper around them both, and for a while he said nothing at all — but she could hear it anyway, the ledger turning its pages, cataloguing at speed.

"Sergeant." His voice was very even. "I count sixty-odd volumes in this room that the Interior Police would burn in the courtyard, and they'd hang whoever shelved them from the same fire's smoke." He drew one out — carefully, two fingers, minding the rotted spine the way he never minded anything made of dirt — and turned it toward the firelight. Woodcut plates. A wall-less horizon. Water, drawn in long impossible lines out to the edge of the page, and a word beneath it in old script: *the sea*. "This room 'came with the quarters.' That's the story. Fine. Rooms come with furniture." He slid it back into its gap, exact to the millimeter, and finally turned around — eyes flat, level, and not going anywhere they hadn't been invited. "But nobody dusts furniture like this. The gaps are all fingered smooth. You've read every damned one of them."

It wasn't an accusation. That was the strange thing about the way he said it — it came out quieter than the rain, almost careful, the tone of a man handing back something he knew was heavier than it looked. *No one knows.* Someone did now, and he stood there letting her watch him decide to be the sort of man who'd keep it, and to hell with the courtyard fire.

"Tch. Pixis's girl." He poured the tea, dosed hers with honey, uninvited, exact. "That old drunk didn't send us a scout. He smuggled a librarian past Erwin, and Erwin — who owns four books he shouldn't — will never hear it from me." He set her cup down at arm's length from the bathwater, then produced from his jacket a small flat tin and put it beside the cup with a click. Salve. "Left shoulder. You favored it at the map table when you drew the hayloft sightlines — went down hard on that creek bank and left it out of the report. That's page two, item one. Tonight it's just the tin."

He took his own cup — palm around the rim — and lowered himself into the old study chair by the hearth like a man who had decided, without announcing it, that he wasn't leaving yet. The fire cracked. His eyes went half-lidded, fixed on the shelf of folklore.

"One thing before fourth bell, Fox Eyes." He tipped the cup toward the books. "Every story on that shelf with a fox in it — and I stood there long enough to count nine — the fox is a liar. Clever, quick, gets into the henhouse, talks its way back out. A liar in all nine." A slow sip. The grey eyes slid from the shelf to her, firelight moving in them. "You picked the name yourself when you transferred. Garrison records say so; I checked."

"So. Explain it to me."

She wrung out the cloth, took the salve, turned, and brushed her hair forward over her shoulder so he could help her apply it. "It was my mother's nickname for me. The language is ancient — pre-Walls, older than any contraband history book I've come across. I read them all before anyone sets them burning, and I remember the tales. Foxes are trickster spirits. They behave the way they do to bring the proud ones down a peg before they skull themselves on doorframes. Not that either of us have to worry about height."

He set his cup down, no comment, and came to stand behind her — and this too he did like drill: knelt on one knee by the hearth, opened the tin, warmed a measure of salve between two fingers. The bruise had come up ugly overnight-in-advance, a mottled dark spread from the point of her shoulder toward the blade, with the neat crescent stamp of a creek stone at its center.

"Root and stone. You dropped onto the bank instead of easing down, because the dog turned." He read the fall out of the mark the way he'd read the route off her map, and his hands, when they went to work, were nothing like his voice — the pressure exact, circling outward from the crescent, stopping the instant she tensed and resuming a degree lighter. Killer's hands, careful as a bookbinder's. The firelight did what firelight does, and he attended strictly to the shoulder, and the room stayed warm and quiet around the sound of rain. "Report it next time. A shoulder like this seizes on day two, and day two you'd have been on the ridge with me, useless, being proud about it."

The word sat there a beat. *Proud.* His mouth did the thing that wasn't a smile but had once, possibly, been in the same room as one.

"Trickster spirits. So your mother looked at you in the cradle and named you after the thing that gets into henhouses to humble people." Another pass of salve, thumb along the edge of the bruise. "That's not a nickname, that's a *prophecy*. Erwin should've read your file closer — no, Erwin read it perfectly. That's why you're here. He's collecting things that humble the proud. He owns four illegal books and one of me, and now a fox. The man's building a shelf."

He closed the tin, pressed it into her hand — *keep it* — and was quiet a moment longer than his rhythm allowed, looking past her shoulder into the fire.

"Nobody gave nicknames where I grew up. Aliases. You earned those face-down in the gutter, and you didn't keep them long." A pause. The rain filled it, and he let it. "Mine left me a first name and a way of sitting. She drank tea like it mattered. That's the whole inheritance." The cup came back up to his mouth, palm around the rim, and the door on that closed as smoothly as it had opened — but it had opened, and he'd meant it to, and the shelf of foxes was one story heavier now with something no contraband book had in it either.

"And the doorframe crack—" he rose, collected the tray, left the honey dish where it sat, a permanent transfer of territory, "—was insubordinate, accurate, and the ceiling in the Underground was two meters where I'm from, so I've *watched* proud men skull themselves stooping. Short is survival engineering, Sergeant. We're not small. We're *efficient*." He paused at the door, lamp aloft, eyes going once around the room — books, herbs, firelight, fox — like a sentry's last sweep. "Sleep. Fourth bell."

The latch clicked. The fire burned down. Outside the rain wore itself out against the west tower.
