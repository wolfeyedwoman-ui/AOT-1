# Chapter 9 — The Morning Ledger

*(in progress)*

Morning arrived the way it always did at the old keep — grey through the west window, rooks arguing on the battlements, the smell of banked embers and cold herbs.

Levi was, of course, already awake. Had been for hours; the man slept like a rumor — two hours in a chair and called it a night. He'd claimed the study chair at some point before dawn, boots on, cravat re-tied to the exact millimeter, and the good teapot was already breathing steam because some things in this world could be relied upon. He had the list open against his knee and was writing in it, pen scratching, upright rows of pins.

From the bed, upside down, one yellow eye cracked open just far enough to read the newest entry as he blotted it:

*"Teeth. — See: Fox. Ledger balanced."*

"You're awake," he said, without looking up, in the tone of a man noting the time. He folded the list, filed it in the breast pocket — patting it once, flatly, *daring* her — and poured the second cup, honey dosed, exact, setting it on the apothecary table at arm's reach from the bed. Then Captain Levi stood, and daylight rules came on with him like a lamp being lit: posture, jacket, the working register.

"Today, Sergeant. Three items." One finger. "The tin comes back — full noon, the mess, maximum audience. You'll be busy eating; let him make the trip to you. Every word to my desk within the hour." Two. "Courier's due through the fork by midday. If Erwin read the letters the way Erwin reads letters, there'll be an answer riding under a requisition stamp — nobody touches the pouch but me." Three — and here he paused at the door, hand on the bolt, and the register dropped one last degree for exactly one sentence, the way it did.

"The vow on page two applies today and all days. West rotation rides tomorrow at dawn — *both* of us. Whatever the woods say at night, Fox Eyes, you don't go hear it alone anymore."

The bolt never made it. Fifty kilograms of fox left the bed at a dead sprint and hit him at the door — and Captain Levi Ackerman, humanity's strongest soldier, absorbed the impact the way a wall absorbs weather: one half-step of pivot, zero sound, hands arriving exactly where they needed to be before his brain had finished filing the requisition. Legs locked around his waist. Arms over his shoulders. Face buried in his neck, curls everywhere, her breath warm against the fresh-tied cravat that had been, until three seconds ago, at the exact millimeter.

Levi stood there holding her with the put-upon expression of a man whose morning briefing had been boarded by pirates.

"Sergeant." Flat. Load-bearing. "I have a regiment."

She did not dismount. The rooks argued on the battlements. Somewhere below, the keep was waking up without its captain, and its captain — this was the part no court-martial would ever hear evidence of — was not putting her down. One forearm had settled under her like a shelf built to code, sure and unhurried, and after a moment, in the privacy of a bolted tower at grey dawn, the other hand came up and pressed once, briefly, against the back of her head. His chin lowered by two degrees until it rested against her curls. Breath went in; breath went out. One breath longer than efficiency permitted. Two.

"*Ambush, domestic, pre-muster,*" he recited quietly over her shoulder, in the cataloguing voice, though notably neither hand moved to reach for the pen. "Filed under A. The list is becoming a *biography*, Fox Eyes."

Then — because dawn was burning and the tin and the courier and the whole ugly chess of the day were waiting under the tower, and because if he stood here one more minute the regiment was going to lose its captain to a shelf of contraband folklore and a woman who smelled of lavender and wood-smoke — he crossed the room in four even steps with her still attached, lowered her onto the bed with the same terrifying gentleness he used on teacups, and pressed the honey-dosed cup into her hands like a writ.

"Noon. The tin. Maximum audience." The grey eyes held hers a last half-second, and whatever moved through them didn't make it into the flat of his voice, but it didn't have to; it had a filing system all its own by now. "Drink your tea while it's hot. That's the only order you've never once disobeyed."

Door. Bolt. Stairs — silence, silence, silence — and then, at the last landing, one floorboard, pressed once, on purpose.

*The signature.*

---

She bathed, dressed, and took the spiral stairs down into a keep already humming toward midday — tack squeaking in the yard, Gunther's inventory voice rolling out of the stores, the mess filling with the noon shift's noise and the smell of yesterday's stew stretched into today's soup.

Full noon. Maximum audience. She drew a bowl, took the squad table's fox-flank seat that had somehow become hers, and ate — visibly, unhurriedly, a soldier with nothing in the world on her schedule. Petra was three seats down darning a strap and radiating the alertness of a pointer pretending to be asleep. Eld had angled himself, coincidentally, where he could see the door.

Grimes arrived on schedule, because his kind always did.

"Sergeant." Warm gravel, full performance, pitched to carry — and the tin came down on the table in front of her with a soft clack, scrubbed to a shine it had never known in its working life, polished like a regimental decoration. The bowl and jug and bottle followed from the crook of his arm, arranged in a neat little parade. "Returned with interest. Your recipe worked — cuff's like the rabbit never happened." He flicked the bangs back, boyish, and swung a leg over the bench across from her without being asked. The room's eyes, feeling the theater, drifted over; he let them. He *fed* on them. "And since I pay my debts—" he leaned in, voice dropping to the confidential half-register while remaining, expertly, just loud enough for the nearest ears to strain at, "—a little something I caught, as promised."

He turned his cup slowly on the boards. The grey-blue eyes came up under the fallen bangs and fastened on her face — and she understood, before the words came, that whatever he said next wasn't the gift. Her *face* was the price. The watching was the transaction.

"Fellow at the depot again. Seems somebody out west is asking — quiet, off the books, coin behind it — about *riders*. Two of them, seen or half-seen, near the fork at first light, yesterday." Turn, turn, went the cup. "No uniforms mentioned. Just riders. Odd thing to pay for, wouldn't you say? Wagon taxes being *so* boring." A beat, silk-smooth. "Thought of you straightaway, of course — you being out that way, hunting. Might want to mind where you ride at dawn, Sergeant. If somebody's buying eyes on that road, somebody's *watching* it."

The mess clattered on around them. Súile spooned her soup and gave him the treeline face — the one that had watched a smugglers' dog turn at forty meters and hadn't moved a muscle — and somewhere behind it, cold and quick, the arithmetic ran: *either the barn crew found sign after all and the principal is fishing for who rode past, or nothing was seen and this is a lure built to make a guilty face flinch.* Both roads led through the man across the table, watching her eat with the patience of a snare.

She gave him nothing. Not a blink's worth. And she watched *him* pay for it: the sixteenth-note lag before he chuckled, letting the silence be charming, letting the offer just — sit.

Then something in him shifted gears, one tooth's worth, and his hand came up and *smoothed* the bangs back — not the flick. The other gesture. Slow, deliberate, both temples, the sandy hair pinned flat under his palm, and with it the whole geometry of his face changed: the boyishness drained out, the lines of the chain-smoked years stood up like scars, and the grey-blue eyes held hers from a face that had stopped asking to be liked.

"You're a hard woman to do favors for, Fox Eyes." Quiet now. Just for her. The nickname, deployed for the first time, sat wrong in his mouth — a thing he'd picked up and used without buying it. "Careful hunting, all the same. Dawn's a lonely hour, and the woods—" the smile returned, but it arrived after the eyes had already left, "—the woods have eyes in them lately."

He rose, collected his shine of a tin— no. He left the tin. He left all of it, the whole neat little parade, on her table like a calling card, straightened his pressed jacket, and strolled out through the noon crowd, greeting two troopers by name on the way, a man among friends.

Through the mess window, in the yard: dust rising off the east road, a courier's horse blowing hard at the gate, the pouch coming off the saddle — and Levi already there, materialized out of nowhere as he did, signing for it with one hand while the other rested, casual as weather, on the pommel of nothing at all. He didn't open the pouch in the yard. He turned, found the mess window across the whole noisy distance, found *her* through it — and tilted his head one centimeter toward the keep.

*Office. Now. Both matters.*
