# Chapter 4 — The Thread

"Last time Petra measured, I'm 152 centimeters on the dot," Súile said, "and I'll skip the honey for his cup, because I don't need farmers' daughters gawking at me while I'm on the road."

"Petra *measured* you." Levi filed that image away — the regiment's sweetest soldier with a chalk line and a suspiciously motivated interest in the standings — and let the honey jab pass with nothing but a slow blink that said it had been received, entered into the list, and scheduled. "Report."

And she gave it — and this was the thing about Fox Eyes that the mess hall never saw, the reason Erwin had signed the transfer papers over three senior officers' objections: the brat evaporated. Posture square, voice level, no wasted words. Fresh ruts leaving the west road two kilometers before the fork, heavy wagons riding low, turning onto a logging track that the survey maps listed as disused. She'd taken the detail off-route and followed at distance. An old timber barn, chinks of lantern light after dark, four men who moved like soldiers and dressed like they weren't. Crates coming off the wagons — she'd gotten close enough, alone, on foot, in the rain, to see the stenciling on one before the storm turned murderous. Military supply stamps. The size and fittings of the long crates wrong for grain, right for maneuvering gear. She'd marked bearings, counted horses, memorized faces, and withdrawn without contact. Then the storm. Then the wagon. Then the mud on his floor.

Levi had gone very still somewhere around *military stamps*. By the end, the tea sat forgotten — and anyone who knew him knew what forgotten tea meant.

"Government-issue gear moving at night off the survey routes." His voice had dropped into its working register, quiet and even. "I've seen that trade from the other end, Sergeant. In the Underground we paid triple for gear with the stamps filed off, and it came down the stairs through men who moved like soldiers and dressed like they weren't." He crossed to the map table, lamp in hand. "Which is why this doesn't go to the Military Police — there's a decent chance it *is* the Military Police. It goes to Erwin, in writing, sealed, and nowhere else. No mess hall talk. Not even the squad, yet."

He rolled the west sector map flat and set the lamp at its corner, then held out the grease pencil to her — handle first, eyes on hers.

"Mark it. Track, barn, sightlines, where you left the detail when you went in close." A pause, and the temperature of his voice shifted one degree — somewhere between commander and the other thing. "Alone, in the dark, in a lightning storm, within rifle range of armed smugglers. We'll be discussing that too. The list has a second page now."

He planted both hands on the table's edge, waiting for her marks. "And at dawn you're riding back out to the fork. With me. Just us — a captain and a sergeant exercising horses. Nobody looks twice." Grey eyes came up under the lamplight, and there it was, buried deep beneath the flat inspection tone, almost invisible: approval, and something warmer than approval. "You found the thread, Fox Eyes. Now show me where it leads."

She drew, and Levi watched her draw — which was its own kind of report.

No hesitation, no corrections. The logging track went down in one clean line, kinked exactly where it crossed the creek. Distances in paces, converted to meters in neat figures. The barn as a rectangle with its long axis marked against north, the lantern-chink wall noted, the approach she'd used hatched in on the downwind side — *downwind*, because there'd been a dog, a detail she added with a small symbol and no commentary, as if going belly-down in freezing mud within earshot of a smugglers' dog was simply a thing Tuesday had required of her. Sightlines fanned out from the hayloft door in thin strokes: whoever held that loft owned the track for four hundred meters.

"Tch." It was his approving *tch* — pitched a note lower than the one for mud. He leaned over the map beside her, close enough that his sleeve brushed her shoulder and stayed there, and tapped the junction where the logging track met the back lanes. "Look at what it sits on. Three farm lanes and a creek crossing, no line of sight from the survey road, and a day's easy cart from the depot at the fork. That barn wasn't found, it was *chosen* — by somebody who reads route maps and knows the patrol schedule. Which means manifests, which means access, which means a uniform somewhere up the chain." He straightened. "Erwin's going to eat this alive. He collects exactly this kind of ugly."

He took paper and pen, wrote — brief, upright strokes, no flourish — sealed it with the Scouts' wings pressed into the wax, and set it aside for the morning courier to Trost, addressed in a way that would pass through no adjutant's hands.

"Dawn, fourth bell. We ride to the fork and no closer — I want the ruts aged, the traffic counted, and your bearings confirmed from the ridge. Two officers exercising horses." He glanced at her sideways. "Wear the plain cloak. The one you never oil. For once your disregard for equipment maintenance is tactically useful."

The lamp had burned low. He walked her to the office door — three steps, made the way he did everything, as if it were drill — and opened it on the dark, rain-quiet corridor. For a moment he simply looked up at her four-centimeter disadvantage in stockinged feet, and the ledger behind his eyes visibly turned its pages: the mud, the *mostly*, the hips, the honey, the going-in-alone-in-lightning.

"The list keeps until the barn's dealt with," he said quietly. "I don't misplace lists, Sergeant. I alphabetize them." A pause. The rain filled it. "You did good work today. The kid's asleep because you were late. Remember that the next time I chew you out for being late, which will be tomorrow if you miss fourth bell."

He handed her the lamp — his lamp, the good one — and turned back to the map table, already reaching for the pen again. From the door, one last flat addendum, without looking up:

"Boots, Fox Eyes. *Rail*. Not the wall this time."
