# Chapter 9 — The Morning Ledger

*(in progress)*

Morning arrived the way it always did at the old keep — grey through the west window, rooks arguing on the battlements, the smell of banked embers and cold herbs.

Levi was, of course, already awake. Had been for hours; the man slept like a rumor — two hours in a chair and called it a night. He'd claimed the study chair at some point before dawn, boots on, cravat re-tied to the exact millimeter, and the good teapot was already breathing steam because some things in this world could be relied upon. He had the list open against his knee and was writing in it, pen scratching, upright rows of pins.

From the bed, upside down, one yellow eye cracked open just far enough to read the newest entry as he blotted it:

*"Teeth. — See: Fox. Ledger balanced."*

"You're awake," he said, without looking up, in the tone of a man noting the time. He folded the list, filed it in the breast pocket — patting it once, flatly, *daring* her — and poured the second cup, honey dosed, exact, setting it on the apothecary table at arm's reach from the bed. Then Captain Levi stood, and daylight rules came on with him like a lamp being lit: posture, jacket, the working register.

"Today, Sergeant. Three items." One finger. "The tin comes back — full noon, the mess, maximum audience. You'll be busy eating; let him make the trip to you. Every word to my desk within the hour." Two. "Courier's due through the fork by midday. If Erwin read the letters the way Erwin reads letters, there'll be an answer riding under a requisition stamp — nobody touches the pouch but me." Three — and here he paused at the door, hand on the bolt, and the register dropped one last degree for exactly one sentence, the way it did.

"The vow on page two applies today and all days. West rotation rides tomorrow at dawn — *both* of us. Whatever the woods say at night, Fox Eyes, you don't go hear it alone anymore."

The bolt never made it. Fifty kilograms of fox left the bed at a dead sprint and hit him at the door — and Captain Levi Ackerman, humanity's strongest soldier, absorbed the impact the way a wall absorbs weather: one half-step of pivot, zero sound, hands arriving exactly where they needed to be before his brain had finished filing the requisition. Legs locked around his waist. Arms over his shoulders. Face buried in his neck, curls everywhere, her breath warm against the fresh-tied cravat that had been, until three seconds ago, at the exact millimeter.

Levi stood there holding her with the put-upon expression of a man whose morning briefing had been boarded by pirates.

"Sergeant." Flat. Load-bearing. "I have a regiment."

She did not dismount. The rooks argued on the battlements. Somewhere below, the keep was waking up without its captain, and its captain — this was the part no court-martial would ever hear evidence of — was not putting her down. One forearm had settled under her like a shelf built to code, sure and unhurried, and after a moment, in the privacy of a bolted tower at grey dawn, the other hand came up and pressed once, briefly, against the back of her head. His chin lowered by two degrees until it rested against her curls. Breath went in; breath went out. One breath longer than efficiency permitted. Two.

"*Ambush, domestic, pre-muster,*" he recited quietly over her shoulder, in the cataloguing voice, though notably neither hand moved to reach for the pen. "Filed under A. The list is becoming a *biography*, Fox Eyes."

Then — because dawn was burning and the tin and the courier and the whole ugly chess of the day were waiting under the tower, and because if he stood here one more minute the regiment was going to lose its captain to a shelf of contraband folklore and a woman who smelled of lavender and wood-smoke — he crossed the room in four even steps with her still attached, lowered her onto the bed with the same terrifying gentleness he used on teacups, and pressed the honey-dosed cup into her hands like a writ.

"Noon. The tin. Maximum audience." The grey eyes held hers a last half-second, and whatever moved through them didn't make it into the flat of his voice, but it didn't have to; it had a filing system all its own by now. "Drink your tea while it's hot. That's the only order you've never once disobeyed."

Door. Bolt. Stairs — silence, silence, silence — and then, at the last landing, one floorboard, pressed once, on purpose.

*The signature.*
